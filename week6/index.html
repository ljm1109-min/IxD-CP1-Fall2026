Programming Design Systems: Introduction

Rune Madsen · programmingdesignsystems.com

01

"We now spend a majority of our time looking at screens instead of paper, and this has created a great need for designers who understand how to design for digital devices."

As someone who studied fine art, I strongly relate to this quote. The author notes that graphic design is rooted in the fine arts, and today even painting and sculpture are shown and experienced through digital spaces. Understanding how to design for digital devices has become necessary for every field, from fine art to industry, and that is one of the reasons I am studying UX design.

02

"Throwing a design over the wall for production is a bad legacy of the printed page, and there is no reason for the design process to end with the birth of a product."

I think UX designers, who constantly iterate throughout the design process, will strongly relate to this quote. Even after many rounds of revision and the launch of a product, we keep discovering problems and continue to improve it. Once a product is out in the world, its impact also reaches beyond individual users to the wider community, so the problems we look for should expand from user problems to social ones.

03

"There is a need for educational material that teaches the fundamentals of graphic design in a modern way, especially if it also gives students mental models for critiquing digital design projects."

I think this is something we should always keep in mind in the age of AI. Now that it has become so easy to produce things, we cannot create good digital design without understanding the fundamentals and developing the ability to critique our work. When I built an app prototype with an AI tool, it generated screens in minutes, but deciding whether they actually worked for the user was still my job.

What Do Prototypes Prototype?

Stephanie Houde & Charles Hill (1997), Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction · PDF

04

"What is significant is not what media or tools were are used to create them, but how they are used by a designer to explore or demonstrate some aspect of the future artifact."

This quote changed my assumption that I always need to show a high-fidelity prototype when presenting to an audience or to people from other departments. What matters is not how polished it looks or which tool I used, but what aspect of the product it helps people explore. I want to start by asking why I am making a prototype and in what context it will be used.

05

"Making separate prototypes enabled specific design questions to be addressed with as much clarity as possible."

Seeing prototypes divided into three dimensions (role, look and feel, and implementation) made it much easier to understand what a prototype is actually meant to show, and I want to apply this to my own work. Until now, I focused only on the final product I had in mind and ran user tests with a single prototype. I now realize that this approach limited what I could discover, because one prototype could not answer every design question clearly.

06

"By focusing on the purpose of the prototype—that is, on what it prototypes—we can make better decisions about the kinds of prototypes to build."

While building my scene, I spent a lot of time trying to fix the character's finger grip on the phone because it looked unnatural. My instructor pointed out that with a low-poly model, exaggeration is expected, and a small change to the phone's angle would be enough. Once I remembered that the purpose was to show context, not anatomy, it became much easier to decide what was worth my time.
